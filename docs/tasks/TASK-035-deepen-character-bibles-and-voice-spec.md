## Public-domain reference boundary

For each ensemble character, select a small **reference constellation** from fictional characters or works whose relevant source text is verified public domain in the United States. Record title, author, publication year, source edition/link, and public-domain basis.

Use references to extract abstract dimensions such as:

- conversational rhythm and social tactic;
- relationship to authority, uncertainty, embarrassment, care, or conflict;
- type of humor and what the person refuses to joke about;
- contradiction between self-image and behavior;
- way of teaching, persuading, deflecting, or apologizing; and
- balance of practical, poetic, formal, blunt, or indirect language.

Blend at least two sources or reference dimensions for each character. Do not copy names, catchphrases, distinctive passages, plots, biographies, identity traits, or a single character's complete personality. Do not write “in the style of” an author, imitate a living performer/adaptation, or quote source dialogue into the game. Public-domain references are analysis inputs, not a substitute for original characterization.

Reference choices are pre-approved, but license/public-domain evidence and originality boundaries still require documentation.

## Character-bible depth

Expand each canonical character with material that can generate behavior even when it never appears directly:

- formative history and path into the current role;
- present living situation, obligations, routines, and non-work interests;
- private ambition, fear, resentment, comfort, and source of pride;
- contradiction, blind spot, coping strategy, and pressure behavior;
- what they notice first in a room and what they routinely miss;
- relationships, debts, affection, rivalry, and different public/private registers;
- vocabulary range, sentence rhythm, metaphor source, humor type, and silence pattern;
- how they speak upward, downward, to peers, and to the protagonist;
- what they will explain patiently, what they assume, and what makes them over-explain;
- personal facts safe for campaign use versus reserved facts that must stay off-page; and
- continuity/art constraints that the prose may not contradict.

Do not make trauma, ethnicity, gender, age, disability, accent, or language fluency a shortcut for voice. A character's technical competence and dignity remain intact even when they are wrong, defensive, funny, or distracted.

## Voice-translation cards

For each of the seven campaign-one speakers, create a practical translation card containing:

- a compact voice fingerprint;
- preferred and avoided constructions;
- three levels of register: ordinary floor conversation, pressure/conflict, and reflective/debrief;
- two or more context-complete candidate payloads translated into that character's language;
- a deliberately bad generic/robotic version and an explanation of why it fails;
- a semantic-drift warning showing what a colorful rewrite must not change; and
- one short exchange with another character that demonstrates relational voice rather than isolated monologue.

The examples must come from TASK-034's context-complete payloads, not obsolete production lines.

## Personal-texture policy

Define a restrained campaign target: roughly five to ten percent of a speaking character's dialogue **moments**, evaluated across the campaign rather than per scene, may carry a personal nugget, off-duty concern, habitual observation, relationship beat, or idiosyncratic joke that only this person would naturally contribute.

This is a texture range, not a mechanical quota. Do not bolt a hobby onto every scene, force private facts into urgent moments, or make personal material compete with safety and technical clarity. Each used character should nevertheless have at least one memorable non-procedural beat across the campaign. Mark which backstory details are available to TASK-036 and which remain iceberg material.
